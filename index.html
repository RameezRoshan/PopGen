The Wahlund Effect & Theoretical Framework

1. What is the Wahlund Effect?

Formulated in 1928 by Swedish geneticist Sten Wahlund, the Wahlund effect states that pooling distinct subpopulations with differing allele frequencies produces an apparent deficit of heterozygotes and an excess of homozygotes compared to Hardy-Weinberg expectations calculated from the average allele frequency.

Crucially, this deficit occurs even if every individual subpopulation is in perfect Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium!

2. Mathematical Derivation

Suppose a total population is divided into \(K\) equal-sized subpopulations, where subpopulation \(k\) has allele frequency \(p_k\). Let \(\bar{p} = \frac{1}{K}\sum p_k\) be the mean allele frequency, and \(\sigma_p^2 = \text{Var}(p) = \frac{1}{K}\sum (p_k - \bar{p})^2\).

$$\bar{P}(A_1A_1) = \bar{p}^2 + \sigma_p^2$$ $$\bar{P}(A_1A_2) = 2\bar{p}\bar{q} - 2\sigma_p^2$$ $$\bar{P}(A_2A_2) = \bar{q}^2 + \sigma_p^2$$

Because the variance \(\sigma_p^2 \ge 0\), the observed heterozygosity in the pooled population is always reduced by exactly \(2\sigma_p^2\). This quantity is known as the Wahlund Heterozygosity Deficit (\(\Delta H\)).

3. Wright's Hierarchical F-Statistics

Sewall Wright partitioned genetic variation into hierarchical inbreeding coefficients:

$$(1 - F_{IT}) = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})$$
  • \(F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T} = \frac{\sigma_p^2}{\bar{p}\bar{q}}\): Fixation index measuring subpopulation differentiation due to drift and limited gene flow.
  • \(F_{IS} = \frac{H_S - H_I}{H_S}\): Inbreeding coefficient of individuals relative to their local subpopulation (non-random mating).
  • \(F_{IT} = \frac{H_T - H_I}{H_T}\): Total inbreeding coefficient relative to the entire pooled population.
